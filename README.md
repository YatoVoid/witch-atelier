# Atelier

A spell-circle workbench in the style of Witch Hat Atelier's magic system. Pick an element, draw signs freehand anywhere on the circle, close the ring, and read what the composition resolves to. Casting is disabled until the ring is actually closed, same as the readout has always warned.

No build step. No backend. Static HTML/CSS/JS, deployable as-is to GitHub Pages.

## Run locally

```
python3 -m http.server 8000
```

Open `http://localhost:8000`.

## How it works

Nothing is pre-selected before you draw a sign. You draw one or more strokes anywhere on the circle (a pause of a second and a half locks the sign in, so a few strokes drawn close together in time count as one sign, matching how most of the reference glyphs are actually built from a spine plus a tick or two), and the shape decides which family it belongs to:

- straight, drawn outward: Column, Crosshair, Enlarge, or Levitation
- straight, drawn inward: Pull
- wide sweep across many angles, outward: Dispersion, Radial, Rain, Billowing, or Weave
- wide sweep, inward: Convergence, Window, or Collection
- gentle back-and-forth wiggle that doesn't travel far from where it started: Float, Dancing Puppet, or Vision
- sharp zigzag: Bolt, Bend, Direction, or Bird
- closed loop, drawn smoothly: Diamond, Repetition, or Eye
- closed loop, drawn chaotically: Crush

There's no shape difference between the signs within a family in the source material either. The stroke narrows it to a family; the sign-list row shows the rest of that family in a dropdown so you can say which one you actually meant, rather than the app pretending to detect a distinction that isn't there in the drawing.

- `js/engine/classify.js`: reads the geometry of a sign's strokes and returns a family and its default member. Three different techniques, for what each is actually good at. Closedness (does the stroke loop back on itself) is read directly off the raw geometry, since it's fundamentally about where the stroke sits relative to the ring, not its own shape. Direction (outward vs inward, Column vs Pull, Dispersion vs Convergence) compares the ring-center distance of the longest single stroke's start to its end, not the first drawn stroke's start to the last drawn stroke's end: a decoration (a cap, a tick, an arrowhead flourish) is often its own separate stroke, and whichever end of the whole gesture it happens to land nearest to has nothing to do with which way the sign was actually drawn, so a decoration landing near the inner end after an unambiguously outward main line used to flip the whole sign to read as drawn inward. Everything else, telling a straight line from a single corner from a multi-turn zigzag from a gentle wiggle, is answered with a small point-cloud shape matcher (the $1 Unistroke Recognizer approach: Wobbrock, Wilson & Li, 2007) instead of counting corners and comparing segment lengths. The drawn stroke (or strokes concatenated in drawing order, unless one stroke already accounts for most of the ink, in which case just that one, for the same decorated-strokes-can-mislead-a-naive-join reason as direction above) is normalized (resampled to even spacing, recentered, rescaled, rotated to a consistent starting orientation) and compared against a handful of reference examples per shape in `js/data/templates.js`, then matched to whichever it's closest to after also searching for the best-fitting small rotation. Comparing the whole shape as a point cloud is far less sensitive to hand tremor and to decoration than a threshold on any single measurement (segment dominance, corner count) ever was, since averaging distance over many points is exactly what cancels noise a lone threshold can't. Only when that match isn't confident (its distance to the closest template is still fairly large) does angular spread around the ring's center get checked at all, to catch a genuine wide sweep: checked after, not before, since a peak or a zigzag can span just as wide an angle from the ring's center as an actual sweep once its arms are long enough, without being any less obviously a corner or a zigzag. Deterministic, no network call, no trained model file, though `classifyStrokeGroup()` does take an optional second argument of extra templates (see `js/training.js` below). Direction (outward/inward, and by extension Dispersion/Convergence and Column/Pull) isn't trainable the way shape is: it's a straight geometric comparison, not a template match, so there's nothing for an example to teach it; get it wrong and the fix has to be in the geometry itself. Signs are meant to be drawn at any size, not just one reference size (a sign drawn larger is a real part of the source material's magic, not just a visual choice, see `compose.js` below), so both the direction check and the shape matcher are tested at size extremes, not just one canonical scale: the angular-spread dead zone around ring center (`SPREAD_DEAD_ZONE`) is small (20px) rather than the 60px an earlier version used, since a large dead zone made a genuinely small Dispersion or Convergence arc unrecognizable outright (every one of its points landed inside the zone, so it could never register any spread at all); and the closed-shape resample count scales with the shape's own perimeter instead of a fixed 20, since a fixed count sampled a small Diamond's corners at proportionally tighter spacing than a large one, making the same few px of hand tremor a much bigger share of the gap between samples. A Diamond drawn genuinely tiny (under roughly 50px across) is still not reliably recognized: below that, hand tremor is 15-40% of the shape's own size, big enough to fake or hide a corner outright rather than just blur one, and no resample-count or smoothing adjustment tried closed that gap without also hurting Crush detection at normal sizes. Tracked as an accepted limitation (`test/run.js` has a regression test at that size with a correspondingly low bar), not silently left unmeasured. A multi-stroke sign where several strokes are comparably sized (a Crosshair's four arms, none dominant enough to shape-match alone) used to always fall to concatenating every stroke into one polyline for matching, but a real hand rarely returns to the exact same pixel between arms, and that small realistic gap between one arm's end and the next arm's start reads to the point-cloud matcher as a corner that was never actually drawn; a Crosshair with four individually near-perfect straight arms could still end up matching nothing confidently once concatenated, and fall through to whatever the least-bad guess happened to be (reported in practice as reading as Bend). Checked first now: if the strokes actually radiate from a shared point (at least 3 of them share a near-common endpoint, distinguishing a genuine multi-arm sign from an ordinary corner or zigzag drawn with the pen lifted partway through, which only ever joins two strokes at a time), each arm gets matched independently and the majority vote wins, without ever concatenating across that gap at all.
- `js/data/templates.js`: the shipped reference examples for the shape matcher above, one array of points per shape. `straight`, `bend`, `bolt`, and `wavy` each carry at least one template traced directly off the actual reference glyph art in `assets/signs/*.webp` (the art was thinned to a skeleton and the resulting point path checked by eye against the source image before being added), alongside the clean geometric templates and the real hand-drawn examples (a line with an arrowhead and a separate crossbar; a crosshair drawn as four separate strokes, traced from an actual screenshot) that were already there. The `bend` and `bolt` templates used to be built from a verbal idea of each shape (a bend is "a corner", a bolt is "a zigzag") rather than the art itself, and didn't actually resemble it: the real Bend glyph is a smooth J-curve, not the symmetric geometric "V" the old template used, and the real Bolt glyph has a small diamond in the middle of its spine that a plain zigzag template had no way to match. That mismatch, not a bad threshold, was why Bend and Direction kept getting confused with each other and with Bolt no matter how the match-distance cutoff was tuned. Real-art and clean-geometric templates are kept side by side rather than one replacing the other: a template traced off actual artwork carries its own small asymmetry, honest to the source but a worse match for a clean, careful hand-drawn corner or zigzag than a template built to be exactly that shape, so both coexist and the matcher takes whichever a given stroke is actually closest to. Add more examples here to improve recognition on a shape that's still getting misread; each only needs to be a representative point sequence, since normalization handles size, position, and rotation.
- `js/training.js`: personal corrections, saved to `localStorage`. If a sign reads as the wrong family, the "Wrong reading?" panel on its row in the sign list (all 8 families, not only the 4 the shape matcher trains on; the other 4 are read from geometry and say so rather than pretending to remember a correction they can't) saves the actual drawn stroke as an extra template tagged with the shape you meant, layered on top of `templates.js` rather than editing it (the same way a saved spell in the grimoire doesn't touch the canon spellbook), and used on every classification after that, on this device, without needing to touch the shipped file. `js/app.js` also offers a one-time calibration flow on first visit (no saved training data yet and not previously dismissed): draw 5 examples of each of the 4 trainable shapes up front instead of only ever learning from corrections after a misread.
- `js/data/signatures.js`: recognizes a drawn spell as a named spellbook entry, but only for the sixteen whose actual sign composition is confirmed: Grasping Wind and Sylph Shoes Seal from the wiki; the rest read directly off their reference art. Two shapes show up repeatedly: a sigil ringed by one straightOut-family sign repeated (the Watershot Seal / Rising Wave radiant pattern, also found built from fire on Pyreball Seal, crystal on Crystal Shard Seal, wind underfoot on River Ferry Seal, and a shorter water ring on Saltwater Bolt Seal), and a sigil ringed by alternating wavy-family signs, Levitation and Float or Bird (Floating Drops and Floatglow Lamp on light, Torrential Flow Seal and Vapor Bubble on water), plus two mixed straightOut-and-wavy rings (Carousel of Lights on light, Rising Platform of Water on water). Light Beam uses the same ring geometry (four Column signs at the cardinal points) and is still detected as part of this pattern, but the wiki documents its actual effect as a single steady beam pointing up rather than a radiant burst, a case where the geometric reading and the documented in-story effect disagree; see the note on its spellbook entry. All 104 spellbook images (the original 40 plus 64 pulled from a second batch of clearer reference redraws) were surveyed for this; the rest either use a motif outside the 8 sigils and 24 signs (faces, creatures, decorative borders, nested nonstandard geometry) or weren't legible enough to be confident about, so there's nothing honest to match against for them. Faking a match would be worse than not detecting them.
- `js/data/sigils.js`: the 8 elements (fire, water, earth, light, wind, wind underfoot, aeriforms, crystal).
- `js/data/signs.js`: the 24 sign archetypes, matching the named signs documented on the wiki. Each one has a `contribute(accumulator, instance)` function describing how it affects direction, spread, sustain, or raw intensity. Where the wiki doesn't document a sign's function (Bird, Eye, Vision, Dancing Puppet, Window), the comment says so, it's a placeholder grouped by feel, not a sourced fact. Which family a sign belongs to (`SIGN_BUCKETS` in `classify.js`) is decided by its own reference glyph's actual shape, not by what its effect resembles: Direction's glyph is a bare peak with no straight spine at all, unlike Pull's (a mostly straight line with a small arrowhead), so it lives with Bend/Bolt (the zigzag family), not with Pull, even though "always pulls toward the seal" sounds Pull-shaped. Levitation, Bird, and Eye used to be filed under "wavy" the same way, on the strength of "float/drift/hover" sounding wave-like, but none of their glyphs are: Levitation's is a plain straight arrow, Bird's dominant curve behaves like Bend's single hook, and Eye's is a closed oval with a center dot, so all three now live in the family their actual glyph shape lands in (straightOut, zigzag, and closedSmooth respectively). Vision (an eight-arm radial burst) and Dancing Puppet (a ring of four loops) are still left under "wavy" despite not being wavy-shaped either, since neither one reliably matches any of the app's eight family detection methods at all; see the comment on `SIGN_BUCKETS` for why forcing a bucket on those two would just trade one wrong answer for a differently fragile one.
- `js/data/spellbook.js`: 104 named canon spells for the reference gallery. Descriptions are only included where the wiki documents what the spell actually does or the composition is confirmed (see `signatures.js` above); the rest show image and name only rather than a guessed description.
- `js/engine/compose.js`: pure function that reduces a chosen sigil + drawn signs into resolved parameters (direction, magnitude, spread, sustain, intensity) and an honest readout, including misfire warnings when directional signs cancel out. Three or more directional signs spread most of the way around the ring are read as a radiant, beacon-like pattern instead of a misfire, since they net to zero by construction (they're pushing outward in every direction at once), the same geometric shape as Light Beam's four Columns at the cardinal points, though Light Beam's actual documented effect is a single beam, not a radiant burst; see spellbook.js. A single sign's own contribution to `intensity` tops out around 0.7 (a sign's length is clamped to 1.4, and a directional sign contributes length * 0.5), so the label's old "high intensity" threshold of 1.2 was mathematically unreachable by one sign alone, no matter how large it was drawn: the label text read identically for the smallest and largest possible version of the same single-sign spell, even though the underlying number did move. Lowered to 0.5, matching the threshold `spreadRatio` and `sustainRatio` already use, so drawing a sign larger, or combining two or three modest ones, can actually cross it.
- `js/engine/render.js`: canvas rendering. The ring and sign strokes are drawn from your actual recorded points, in the same dark red ink sampled from the reference artwork (`assets/sigils/fire.webp`, rgb(108,0,0)), not plain black. The sigil glyph is drawn from an image (see below). The cast animation gives each element an organic shape with a comet trail (a licking flame for fire, a falling droplet for water, a curling gust for air, a tumbling shard for earth/crystal, a glowing ray for light) instead of generic dots and rectangles.
- `js/engine/vector.js`: angle and compass-bearing math. Canvas angles put 0 degrees at east with y growing downward; `toBearing()` converts that to a map-style compass, 0 degrees at north, increasing clockwise, since that's what the readout displays.
- `js/grimoire.js`: localStorage persistence and spell-code export/import. A spell is fully described by its data, so sharing one is just sharing a string, no server needed.

Direction and strength are computed, not looked up: each directional sign contributes a force vector from its angle and drawn length, and the net direction/skew come from summing those vectors. Nothing about a specific element x sign combination is hardcoded. `compose.js` assembles the readout label from whatever generic parameters the placed signs happen to produce.

## The artwork

The sigil and sign glyphs in `assets/` are hand-drawn reconstructions of the canon designs, not traced from the manga's published pages. The spellbook gallery images are the same. This project doesn't reproduce the source material's actual printed artwork; that's copyrighted, and it also isn't available as data this tool could read even if that were the intent.

## Adding a new sigil or sign

This is the part meant to grow as more of the source material's rules get pinned down.

- **New element**: add an entry to `SIGILS` in `js/data/sigils.js` (name, particle style, image path) and put the image at that path. Nothing else changes.
- **New sign archetype**: add an entry to `SIGN_ARCHETYPES` in `js/data/signs.js` with a `contribute()` function and image path, and add it to the right family (or a new one) in `SIGN_BUCKETS` in `js/engine/classify.js`.
- **New spellbook entry**: add a row to `SPELLBOOK` in `js/data/spellbook.js` with an image path, and a `description` only if the wiki actually documents what it does.

## Testing the recognition engine

`test/run.js` is a plain Node script, no dependencies, no build step: it loads `templates.js`, `classify.js`, `signatures.js`, and the rest of the engine the same way `index.html` does (as sequential globals) and drives them with synthetic stroke geometry instead of a real pointer. It checks every sign family at several ring positions and orientations, since more than one past bug was position- or orientation-specific, a jitter pass (a shape has to hold up as the majority read across many noisy variants at a plausible hand-tremor level, not just classify correctly when drawn geometrically perfectly) including small-size variants of Dispersion, Convergence, and Diamond specifically (since a fixed absolute jitter amount hits a small sign proportionally much harder than a large one), three real hand-drawn examples traced from an actual misclassification (including one pulled verbatim from a user's own devtools console rather than reconstructed by eye from a screenshot, after an earlier reconstruction attempt failed to reproduce the bug it was meant to catch), a check that a single sign's intensity reading actually changes visibly in the label text when drawn larger, not just in the underlying number, and every entry in `SPELL_SIGNATURES` against its exact recipe and a handful of near-miss recipes that should be rejected. Run it with `node test/run.js`.

## Deploying to GitHub Pages

1. `git init`, commit, push to a GitHub repo.
2. In the repo's Settings, Pages, set the source to the `master` branch, root directory.
3. No Actions workflow needed since there's no build step.
